# Thematic Analysis — Post-Task Interviews

*Braun & Clarke hybrid deductive/inductive thematic analysis of semi-structured post-task interview transcripts. 28 of 35 analyzed participants have a transcript (missing: P1, P13, P2, P4, P6, P9, PCD5 — not interviewed or not yet transcribed). 15 Explícita, 13 Implícita.*

## 1. Method

**Interview structure** (four core questions, with adaptive follow-ups based on each answer):
1. *"Como foi para si jogar com o robô?"* (How was it for you to play with the robot?)
2. *"Como descreveria o papel do robô no jogo / o que é que ele estava aqui a fazer?"* (How would you describe the robot's role / what was it doing here?)
3. *"Quando o robô interagiu consigo, o que lhe passou pela cabeça?"* (When the robot interacted with you, what went through your mind?)
4. *"Então o robô foi útil ou atrapalhou? O que achou mais útil/atrapalhou mais?"* (So was the robot useful or did it get in the way? What was most useful/most got in the way?)

**Coding approach.** A small deductive starter codebook was built around the study's constructs (self-attributed vs. externally-attributed ownership, robot role framing, affect, usefulness/hindrance), while remaining open to inductive codes for anything unanticipated. **Limitation to flag honestly**: coding was not done blind to condition (participant condition was visible throughout, for practical reasons) — code assignment should be read as a single analyst's structured first pass, not independently verified or blind-coded. Counts per code are small (often 1) and are reported to show *pattern direction*, not tested statistically.

All 28 transcripts were combined with explicit participant/condition headers before coding, specifically to guarantee correct attribution of every quote to the right participant and condition.

## 2. Themes

### 2.1 Self-attribution of responsibility — the most H1-relevant pattern found

Participants frequently framed errors, corrections, and struggle as **their own** doing rather than the robot's — but this happened far more often in **Implicit** transcripts.

| | Implícita | Explícita |
|---|---|---|
| Self-attributed correction/error/difficulty instances | **7** (M1, P3, PA11, PA6, PA8, PCD2, +1) | **2** (M2, PA2) |

Representative quotes:
- PCD2 (Implícita): *"Não me atrapalhou, não. Eu é que me atrapalhei."* ("It didn't get in my way. I was the one who got in my own way.") — the clearest instance in the dataset.
- PA6 (Implícita): *"Que eu que estava a fazer errado... E que tinha de corrigir."* ("That I was the one doing it wrong... and that I had to correct it.")
- P3 (Implícita): *"Dá as suas pistas... só que a minha memória é que não está boa."* ("It gives its clues... it's just that my memory isn't good.")

This is a genuinely direct piece of qualitative evidence for H1: participants in the Implicit condition more often describe themselves — not the robot — as the agent responsible for both catching and fixing problems, consistent with preserved self-attributed ownership over the task.

### 2.2 Usefulness mechanism tracks the actual hint style — a qualitative manipulation check

Almost every participant in both conditions affirmed the robot was useful (see 2.3), but **what they described as useful differed by condition** in a way that lines up with the manipulation itself:

- **Implicit participants** described usefulness in **indirect, discovery-oriented** terms: spatial/directional cues (M4: *"da direita, da esquerda, do meio"*), memory-jogging (PA3: *"a ajuda que veio avivar-me a memória"*), verification (M1: *"posso me enganar, que ele ratifica"*), discovery (PA6: *"a descobrir onde é que era"*).
- **Explicit participants** described usefulness in **direct, compliance/accuracy-oriented** terms: direct placement (M2: *"ajudar-me a pôr as cores no sítio certo"*), accuracy/trust (PA13: *"não me enganou"*), task-compliance (P14: *"aquilo que ele me pediu"*).

This convergence — participants' own account of *how* they were helped matching the actual condition assignment — functions as an independent, qualitative confirmation of the same manipulation the quantitative manipulation check already validated.

### 2.3 Near-ceiling positivity on "useful vs. hindering"

Explicit hindrance was rare: only PA2 (*"só quando foi os 2 no mesmo"* — only when two cues came at once) and PCD1 (sequence-length memory overload, see 2.5) described the robot as getting in the way in any substantive sense, and PA4's *"mandá-lo calar"* (tell it to be quiet) was immediately walked back by the participant when probed. This matches the quantitative manipulation check almost exactly (Implicit M=4.61, Explicit M=1.82 on "I still had to figure it out," but *both* conditions rated the robot as helpful on the useful/hindering item) — worth citing as convergent evidence rather than a new finding.

### 2.4 Active independent problem-solving under indirect hints (Implicit-specific)

- PA10: *"Eu tava a ver se conseguia adivinhar."* ("I was trying to see if I could guess.")
- PA3 describes working out the answer through memory-jogging cues rather than being told directly.

Direct qualitative evidence of participants actively inferring answers rather than being handed them — the behavioral mechanism H1 depends on.

### 2.5 Concrete, actionable design feedback

- **PA2 (Explícita)**: overlapping/simultaneous cues were confusing — a specific, fixable interface issue.
- **PCD1 (Explícita)**, who disclosed a memory impairment from a head injury: by the time later hints arrived, earlier sequence items were already forgotten — *"quando chegava à 4ª [cor], já me dava a primeira [esquecida]."* This directly echoes the quantitative finding that hint timing interacts with sequence length, and is a concrete design lead for adapting hint pacing to individual memory capacity.

### 2.6 Personification

Common in both conditions (P8, PA5, PA15, PA8, PCD1): several participants spontaneously described the robot "as if it were a person," including PCD1's *"tava ali uma pessoa que tava realmente a olhar para aquilo... eu já tava estranha"* (mild discomfort alongside the personification). Not a condition-differentiator, but relevant context for how socially participants engaged with the platform.

### 2.7 Cognitive/memory impairment disclosures

Several participants spontaneously disclosed relevant context: Alzheimer's (PA10), a head injury affecting memory (PCD1), low prior schooling (PA11, only to 3rd grade), and self-described slow comprehension (PCD6, P12). Spread across both conditions — important sample context for interpreting difficulty-related codes, not a condition effect.

### 2.8 One-off but notable: desire for reciprocal interaction

PA9 (Explícita) was the only participant to spontaneously raise wanting the robot to respond back in conversation: *"Eu não interajo com as perguntas... porque nunca se pronunciou quando eu [disse algo]."* A distinct UX observation about one-directional vs. two-directional interaction expectations, worth keeping in mind for future iterations even though it's a single instance here.

## 3. Summary for the paper

The strongest, most directly usable finding is **2.1** (self-attribution) — it's the clearest qualitative counterpart to H1, showing up as a real difference in how participants narrated responsibility for errors and corrections, not just in quantitative scale scores. **2.2** is a good supporting citation for manipulation validity. **2.3** and the near-universal usefulness ratings support H2 (no cost to perceived task support). **2.5** offers two concrete, low-cost design improvements for future iterations, independent of condition.

## 4. Limitations

- Not blind-coded; single analyst; no second coder or inter-rater reliability check.
- 7 of 35 participants missing transcripts — check whether this is a systematic gap (e.g., a particular session type) before treating the 28 as representative.
- Code counts are small; treat frequencies as directional, not as a formal count amenable to significance testing.
- Translations from Portuguese are working translations for this report, not formally back-translated or validated.
